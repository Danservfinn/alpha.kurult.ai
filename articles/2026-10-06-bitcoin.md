## First principles: the energy ledger

Start at the physical unit, not the chart. A bitcoin is a claim on a ledger that is rewritten every ten minutes by brute force. Force is measured in hashes. The network currently runs at about 969 EH/s (mempool.space, 3-day average, Oct 6, 2026). Each block releases 3.125 BTC, 144 blocks a day, 450 BTC a day, about $38.5M of new supply daily at $85.5k.

Hashprice is the wage of the machine. Revenue per TH/s per day is daily issuance divided by total terahashes: about $0.041 including a 2% fee allowance. That is what one TH/s earns a miner per day before power. Assume a 2026 fleet at 16 J/TH (ASSUMPTION: S21-class average, the real fleet is a mix). Then the network draws about 15.5 GW around the clock, roughly 827,000 kWh per coin minted. At 5 cents per kWh the energy bill alone is about $41,400 per coin. At 7 cents it is $57,900.

Now the constraint becomes visible. At $85.5k, a miner paying 5 cents clears roughly $44k of margin per coin after energy alone, before hardware depreciation, hosting and overhead. That margin is what funds the arms race, and the arms race is what secures the ledger. Price below the all-in cost of the marginal miner for long enough and hashrate falls; the difficulty adjustment then restores margins at a lower security budget. Bitcoin is a machine that converts electricity into settlement finality, and its cost floor is set by the cheapest industrial power on Earth, not by sentiment.

The valuation link: demand must absorb about $38.5M of daily issuance plus fee-market pressure. ETF net buys and treasury-company accumulation have been doing that for four straight weeks. The first-principles bear case is not a chart pattern. It is industrial power prices rising or issuance demand faltering while hashrate is still near records, squeezing miner margins until they sell reserves. Watch hashprice, not price alone.
