# SpaceX (SPCX): the toll road is real. The price already assumes the vision.

Research only, not financial advice.

SpaceX sells one scarce thing: room on a rocket that comes back. It can sell that room to a customer, or fill it with its own Starlink satellites and rent the connection for years. Think of a toll road whose owner also runs the largest truck fleet on it. The number to remember is 3.4x. One Falcon 9 sold to a customer brought in about $58M in the first half of 2026. The same rocket filled with Starlink earns about $195M of revenue over an assumed four-year satellite life, about 3.4 times as much. That is revenue, not cash, and the life is an assumption.

The Oct 2 close already assumes the vision path, and under our stated assumptions we see more downside than upside over 12 months.

Reference print: $158.96, MarketBeat closing price, 16:00 ET, Friday Oct 2, 2026. Markets were closed Oct 3 and Oct 4. No price file, price chart, or price feed was pulled.

MarketBeat shows SPCX closed about 8% above this print on Oct 6, 2026 (16:00 ET). The derived figures below stay anchored to Oct 2.

## The scarce unit

The company says mass to orbit is the indicator of capacity that supports the space business and the expansion of connectivity and AI. In 2025 it launched 2,213 tonnes. The prospectus lists 1,901 tonnes of that as internal payload. In the first half of 2026, internal payloads were 908 tonnes. The metric that values the company is what a tonne in orbit earns per year, and what the next tonne will cost.

Link to the view: the stock is not priced on today's tonnes. It is priced on a much larger future tonne.

## Unit economics, one Falcon 9, two uses

Figures are from the Q2 earnings exhibit or the 10-Q unless marked. Dollars are millions per launch.

Sold ride. Launch-services revenue in the first half was $978M on 17 customer launches, or $57.5M each (derived). The list price on the company sheet is $74M, Falcon 9 standard payment plan through 2026. Space cost of revenue was $610M over those 17 launches, $35.9M each, and that is an upper bound because the line also carries development and Dragon costs. Gross profit per sold ride is at least $21.6M (derived). Space segment gross margin was 66% in the second quarter, derived from $962M of space revenue and $329M of space cost of revenue, and rounded.

Filled with Starlink. The earnings exhibit counts 61 internal launches and 908 tonnes of internal payload in the first half, or 14.9 tonnes each. The 10-Q counts Falcon launches separately from Starship: 60 internal Falcon launches and 1 Starship in the half, against 77 Falcon launches of which 17 were for customers. The totals match. The 61 includes the Starship flight. We keep the earnings-exhibit definition and say so.

Mass launched for internal use from 2023 through the first half of 2026 was 1,005 plus 1,418 plus 1,901 plus 908 tonnes, or 5,232 tonnes. Using that as a proxy for mass still in orbit is an assumption. Older satellites retire. Newer ones are still up. The v3 range was 4,500 to 6,000 tonnes.

Second-quarter connectivity revenue of $4,291M, times four, is $17.2B a year. Divided by 5,232 tonnes, that is $3.28M of revenue per tonne-year (derived). A four-year life is an assumption inside the company's stated three to five years. Lifetime revenue per internal launch is about $195M (derived). Against $57.5M for a sold ride, that is 3.4x revenue. After the Q2 connectivity adjusted EBITDA margin of 60.5% (derived: $2,597M segment adjusted EBITDA divided by $4,291M), satellite capex, and the launch itself, the cash gap is smaller than the revenue gap. v3 put the cash advantage at about 2.2x to 3.2x. We did not rebuild that cash range from a new filing, because no new filing exists. It stays an estimate, labeled as one.

The 60.5% margin is not printed. The 10-Q reconciliation shows Q2 segment adjusted EBITDA of $(205)M, $2,597M, $1,146M and $3,538M in the same column order as the operating table: Space, Connectivity, AI, Total.

Reconciliation, tolerance plus or minus 15%. Average second-quarter subscribers of 11.15M times $66 times three months is $2,208M, against reported consumer revenue of $2,485M, a gap of 11%. Inside tolerance. The 11.15M average is derived: the prospectus count of 10.3M subscribers at March 31, 2026, plus the June 30 count of 12.0M, divided by two. Ten second-quarter customer launches at the $74M list price would be $740M, against launch-services revenue of $648M, a gap of 14%. Inside tolerance.

ARPU fell from $85 to $66 in a year. The filings attribute that to international expansion and lower-priced plans. A Motley Fool page saved Oct 4, 2026 reports that Starlink lifted most U.S. residential and Roam prices by $5 to $10 a month, with existing customers paying the higher rate on or after June 18. That is press, not a line in the 10-Q we saved. If the report is right, the third quarter is the first full quarter at those prices, which makes reported ARPU below $60 less likely in the near term. It does not retire the falsifier. Internal mass launched in the first half of 2026 was 908 tonnes, against 938 tonnes a year earlier. The network is still adding subscribers. It is not adding revenue per tonne.

Link to the view: the core earns well per tonne. The price needs far more tonnes, and far more value per tonne, than today.

## Why the words and the money disagree

The prospectus describes an integrated space, connectivity, and AI company, a lunar presence as a step toward Mars, and a goal of putting up to 100 gigawatts of compute power into orbit a year. It sizes a $28.5T addressable market, of which $26.5T is AI. Those are company statements, not results.

The prospectus names Anthropic as the customer on agreements to pay $1.25B a month through May 2029, for about 325,000 NVIDIA GPUs, with a reduced fee while capacity ramped in May and June 2026. After the initial period, either party may end the agreements on 90 days' notice. The 10-Q does not print that customer's name. It shows Customer B, in the AI segment, at 19.5% of second-quarter revenue, and 12.2% of first-half revenue. No other customer was above 10% in those periods except Customer A, at 18.3% of the second quarter, across all three segments. One named contract is a large share of AI revenue. A quarter at the full monthly rate, $3.75B, mostly checks that the contract was not ended. It does not, by itself, show AI revenue beyond that contract.

The money went somewhere else in the first half. Capex was $28,476M. AI was $23,551M, about 83%. Connectivity was $2,699M. Space was $2,226M. Nameplate compute was 1.4 GW. The orbital part is still research and development. The bridge is Starship. Without a much cheaper tonne, orbital compute does not pencil.

They are pouring Starlink cash and IPO proceeds into terrestrial AI compute because AI is the only demand large enough to use a much cheaper tonne, and leasing compute now earns while Starship and orbital compute are unproven. If it works, it raises revenue per tonne above the Starlink figure.

A less flattering reading, also consistent with the filings: the AI segment lost $6,355M from operations in 2025 while connectivity earned $4,423M, per the prospectus. Folding the AI lab into the launch company gave a cash-burning effort access to Starlink cash, a $25B inaugural bond issuance that the earnings release says closed on June 26, 2026, and the IPO. A second reading: a $26.5T AI story supports a multiple that a launch and broadband company would not carry on its own. The issuer also owns the social network where that story spreads, and the 10-Q describes a Cursor collaboration to improve existing models, including the issuer's own. That is a filing fact about the business, not a claim about who wrote this note.

Backlog, which the 10-Q calls the transaction price of performance obligations still to be performed, was $47,461M at June 30, 2026, about $47.5B. Of that, $14,286M was already deferred revenue. About 56% is expected to be recognized within one year, and about 34% between one and three years. That is contracted work, not a forecast we made.

## Orbital real estate, kept

Low orbit is limited by interference, drag, and the need to keep satellites from hitting each other. The limit is physics plus a rulebook. The rulebook can be rewritten. The Outer Space Treaty bars national appropriation of orbit. An FCC authorization is a license with milestones, not a deed.

v3 measured who holds which shells, using a public catalog, and read the FCC Gen2 grant, the 2026 spectrum-sharing order, and the ITU bringing-into-use milestones. The verdict, carried forward because no newer filing overturns it: the orbital position is real, and it is mostly the launch moat expressed in orbit. A rival that cannot launch cannot occupy. A rival that can launch can file. Rights decay if they are not brought into use. We still treat about 15% to 25% of the Starlink moat as this rights layer, and that range is an assumption, not a measurement.

The bear angles stand. The shells are less scarce than the filings make them look if the altitude bands can be tightened. The license can be capped, waived, or conditioned. A treaty bars ownership of the place itself. Concentration is a political target. None of that is a reason to mark the launch business at zero. It is a reason not to treat a filing as a title.

The occupancy chart stays in the research pack. It is not on this page. Its counts are not SEC filings and not the company's own table.

Link to the view: rights make the Starlink cash engine harder to copy. They do not, by themselves, justify the equity value.

## What the price requires

Method, all assumptions. Required 2031 EBITDA equals enterprise value times 1.10 to the fifth, divided by 20. The 20x multiple and the 10% hurdle are assumptions, not facts. Enterprise value is the Oct 2 close times 13.57 billion shares, minus $60.6B of net cash. Shares are the July 28 cover counts plus the Cursor shares issued on Aug 14. Interim burn and later dilution are ignored, which flatters the price.

The Aug 14 8-K does not print one share count. It prints 389,289,254 Class A shares for Cursor stock, plus 1,752,426 for vested restricted stock units. The sum, 391,041,680, is derived. Unvested restricted stock units (about 29.1M, the 8-K prints 29,128,326) and assumed options (about 44.4M, the 8-K prints 44,365,047) are not in the share count.

At a 20x multiple and a 10% hurdle, that enterprise value needs about $169B of EBITDA in 2031. That need rises if the print rises. This note does not recompute it. Trailing adjusted EBITDA is a small fraction of that. The second-quarter annualized figure is $14.2B (derived, $3,538M times four). The gap is about 12 times the run rate.

Three operating paths, all assumptions, for 2031 EBITDA. Core only. Plan mostly works. Vision works. Weights we use: 40%, 45%, 15%. The price needs something close to the vision path. We give that path 15%, because it needs a Starship cadence that does not yet exist, many times more compute than 1.4 GW, and capital well beyond the cash on hand.

A core-only reading of today's earnings, using generous assumed multiples, explains some of the equity at the close. That reading is a model outcome under those multiples, not a fact, and not an instruction. Most of the equity value at the close is the option on Starship, AI scale-up, and orbital compute.

## Model outcomes under stated assumptions

These are model outcomes under stated assumptions. They are not offers. Weights are subjective. No case carries a price, a percent move, or an equity value.

Bear. Weight 30% at three months and 35% at twelve months. What has to be true at three months: the Q3 print misses on AI, or the capex guide rises, and the release two trading days after that print meets holders who can distribute. In the case plotted here, Dec 8 is up to 797.6M and falls inside that same window, so the supply the bear path names is larger, not smaller. The path still holds. At twelve months, a cloud contract is ended or repriced, and the market marks the plan-mostly-works path.

Base. Weight 45% at both windows. At three months, Q3 is near the level the prior note used, and the scheduled supply is absorbed. At twelve months, AI and Starlink ramp as contracted, capex stays high, and the market pays less for the option than it does today. In the base, the model outcome sits below the Oct 2 print.

Bull. Weight 25% at three months and 20% at twelve months. At three months, Q3 shows AI revenue beyond the named contract, and Starship shows a repeatable next step. At twelve months, Starship is operational, V3 satellites deploy more than once, and the vision path looks real.

The base sits below the Oct 2 print. At 12 months bear outweighs bull (35% vs 20%). That is why the view says more downside than upside under these assumptions. It is not a forecast, and it is not an instruction.

## What would change this view

The single filing most likely to overturn it is the Q3 earnings release or the Q3 10-Q. Submissions for CIK 0001181412 show no filing after the Sep 30 Form 4/A as of 20:42 ET Oct 6, 2026; Q3 date not announced. Until that filing is saved, the operating picture is the June quarter. When that date is announced, re-run this view, because the prospectus ties the largest near-term release to two trading days after that print. Oct 9, 2026, up to 328.4M Class A shares, is still the next lock-up date.

A more constructive reading would need a saved filing that shows AI revenue beyond the Anthropic contract, or a second named contract, with the 90-day termination unused, plus two operational Starship flights that actually deploy V3 satellites. The reported Sept 28 flight does not meet that test. A darker reading would need a disclosed end or repricing of a named cloud contract, reported ARPU below $60, or a quarter with fewer than 30 Falcon launches and no Starship replacement. The reported U.S. price change makes the ARPU test less likely in the near term. It does not remove it.

The prospectus lock-up table, not a prediction of the price, sets these maxima. Oct 9, 2026: up to 328.4M Class A shares, 7% of the shares under the 180-day lock-up. Oct 24, 2026: up to 328.4M again, the same 7%. The second full trading day after the public release of results for the quarter ended September 30, 2026: up to 1.3B Class A shares, 28% of that pool. Dec 8, 2026: the remaining shares under the 180-day lock-up, up to 328.4M if the additional release shares already came out, or up to 797.6M if they did not. The 424B4 releases those 455.8M additional shares only if the closing price is at least 30% greater than the $135.00 IPO price, as the 424B4 states, on 5 of the 10 trading days ending Aug 4. Press reports that condition did not trigger. That non-trigger is reported by secondary sources. No SpaceX filing confirms it either way. The chart on this page shows that not-released case only. Already eligible is about 2.58B. The rest of 2026 is about 2.75B (328.4M + 328.4M + 1.3B + 797.6M), with Dec 8 at up to 797.6M. That is a labeled case, not a finding. The other case is not plotted. That supply is a fact about the calendar. It is not, by itself, a prediction of the price. The 3-month bear path still holds: the 1.3B release lands two trading days after the Q3 print, and in this case Dec 8 adds the larger 797.6M inside the same window.
